# ARABIA — two `verbatim_with_provenance` slots, both filled

*Ruling 70 rides on these two entering the index. Both were chosen before that

## A1 — al-Ruwāfa, Nabataean–Greek bilingual, 164/166–169 CE

**Proposed id `arabia-05-ruwafa`.** A temple dedication by the Thamud honouring
**Marcus Aurelius Antoninus and Lucius Aurelius Verus** for the Parthian
victories, naming **Claudius Modestus** and **Quintus Antistius Adventus**.
Al-Ruwāfa, 75 km south-west of Tabūk, north-west Saudi Arabia.

- **Source text and published English translation on one open page.** Giuseppe
  Petrantoni, *Corpus of Nabataean Aramaic–Greek Inscriptions*, Antichistica 28 /
  Studi orientali 11 (Venezia: Edizioni Ca' Foscari, 2021), chapter "Saudi
  Arabia", **entry 47, pp. 121–128**. DOI 10.30687/978-88-6969-507-0/004,
  peer-reviewed, CC BY 4.0, ISBN 978-88-6969-508-7.
- **The URL that works is the per-chapter PDF**, not the book PDF and not the
  HTML chapter page, both of which refuse automated fetching:
  `https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-508-7/978-88-6969-508-7-ch-04.pdf`
- **Loaded and read by this session**, not taken from the hunt: sections **A, C,
  E are Greek and B, D are Nabataean**, each with an English translation
  following, then commentary.
- **The hedge is printed in the edition itself, between named editors.** Milik's
  reconstruction against Macdonald's objection, verbatim from the chapter:
  *"Macdonald does not agree with this reading and, conversely, he points out
  that there are two ṭ"* (p. 126); *"Macdonald suggests that this translation is
  philologically impossible because here ramaṣa is not followed by bayna"*
  (p. 126); *"the initial sequence, which Milik originally read ϹIϹΘAΙΟΙ (on the
  copy of Philby), turns out to be problematic in its interpretation"* (p. 128).
- **GLOSS-CAPABLE, `contested_reading`.** The dispute is between two identifiable
  editors, printed in the reachable edition, and it is about how to READ the
  text — not a translator's hedge about a geographical identification, which is
  the discount ruling 62 had to take on `eastasia-05-liangshu-qin-lun`.
- **The objection, stated rather than left for a reader to raise.** Three of the
  five sections are Greek, and the corpus already carries one Greek text serving
  three regions (FINDING 62). This is not that: the Nabataean sections are the
  region's own language, the speakers are the Thamud, and
  `source_language_provenance` will classify it on `findspot` — al-Ruwāfa is in
  the region. It is a bilingual, which the tier has none of.

## A2 — Safaitic inscription dated by "the year Syllaeus fled from Rome", c. 12–9 BC

**Proposed id `arabia-06-syllaeus`.** A man records his genealogy, three named
dead and a drought, and dates it by the year a man called S¹ly fled from Rome —
identified by the editors as **Syllaeus, the Nabataean minister who guided Aelius
Gallus's Roman expedition into Arabia and was tried and executed at Rome under
Augustus**. Wādī al-Ḥašad, about 45 km north-east of as-Safawi, Jordan.

- **Transliteration, English translation and commentary in one open repository
  PDF**, loaded and read by this session:
  `https://real.mtak.hu/53785/1/062.2017.70.1.2.pdf`
- **Citation.** Nada Al-Rawabdeh & Sabri Abbadi, "The Nabataean Minister Syllaeus
  in a New Ancient North Arabian (Safaitic) Inscription", *Acta Orientalia
  Academiae Scientiarum Hungaricae* 70/1 (2017), 21–30,
  DOI 10.1556/062.2017.70.1.2.
- **Rome is NAMED IN THE TEXT.** The corpus has no other Arabian record of which
  that is true; `arabia-04-jabal-dabub` was flagged in the seed manifest itself
  as "not directly Rome-linked".
- **The hedge is the editors' own and it is about the reading**, verbatim: *"the
  letter y has been added because it does not exist in the inscription, but it is
  clear that the name s¹ly is intended because of the parallel with AbNSJ 1."*
  The identification is separately hedged by the same authors as *"an
  interpretation and certainly not one which can be proven with certainty"* —
  **that second sentence is in their companion paper and was NOT read at source
  by this session.** Use the first; it is on the page that was read.
- **The weakness, recorded.** The inscription has **no corpus siglum** — the
  article calls it "Inscription 1". A record citing it names the article, not a
  corpus number, and that is a durability risk of exactly the kind
  `arabia-04-jabal-dabub` does not carry.
- **A citation trap.** The same authors have a near-identical paper in
  *Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry* 16/2 (2016), 33–40. **Cite the
  Acta Orientalia version**; it is the one verified open.

## Arabia — considered and rejected

- **CIH 541, Abraha's Maʾrib dam inscription, Sabaic, 548 CE.** Ideal content —
  Abraha receives embassies from the Negus, the Roman emperor, the Persian king
  and Arab rulers. **Rejected on reachability: DASI (`dasi.cnr.it`), the standard
  open corpus for Ancient South Arabian, is behind an Anubis anti-bot wall and
  returned "Access Denied" to every route tried.** This closes the whole Sabaic
  vein for now and it is the single most valuable thing lost in this hunt. A
  human with a browser should re-test it.
- **Pompeii's Safaitic graffiti** — nine groups on the plaster behind the
  theatres, late Dec 69 – Jan 70 CE, argued to be Safaitic-writing soldiers with
  Legio III Gallica. **Superb content, and the best unclaimed candidate this hunt
  found.** Rejected because the editio princeps is in *Journal of Roman Studies*
  behind Cambridge's paywall and OCIANA's search is verification-gated, so the
  record URLs could not be retrieved. Worth a human pass.
- **AbSWS 79** (Safaitic, "the Romans caused despair, the year of the Jews") —
  the most explicit naming of Rome among the reachable OCIANA texts, and rejected
  for a reason worth keeping: **DMIPERP states that the OCIANA text itself "is in
  need of correction here and will be updated when possible".** A curator would
  be transcribing a text its own editors flag as wrong. That is a defect, not a
  contested reading, and the two must not be confused.
- **Al-Namārah.H 91; KRS 1023 / KRS 1039; HSNS 4; RSIS 324** — all verified live
  on the legacy Oxford OCIANA (`krc.orient.ox.ac.uk/ociana/corpus/pages/`) with
  transliteration and English translation. All rejected as second-best: each
  touches Rome at one remove, through a Herodian client king or through the
  Jewish war, rather than naming it.
- **Al-Mundhir Paleo-Arabic inscription** (Al-Jallad, al-Manaser & Fisher, 2026;
  the Jafnid al-Mundhir III, Byzantium's ally) — **Wiley returned 403 to every
  automated request**, so open access could not be verified. Strong enough that
  the DOI (10.1111/aae.70013) is worth a human check.
- **Jabal Usays (528/9 CE)** — dated by the **Bostran era**, the era of the Roman
  province, and recording dispatch by al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala, Byzantium's phylarch;
  its year is read as either 528 or 532/3. Rejected: the standard text and
  English translation is Fiema, Al-Jallad, Macdonald & Nehmé in Greg Fisher (ed.),
  *Arabs and Empires before Islam* (OUP 2015), **paywalled**, with no open copy
  confirmed.
- **Ḥarrān Greek–Arabic bilingual (568 CE)** — the one page carrying both text and
  an English translation is an apologetics site whose **translations are entirely
  unattributed**: no translator, no publication, no page. Refused on its face.
- **Zebed trilingual (512 CE)** — the Arabic is a short addition to a Greek–Syriac
  martyrion and the Roman touch is a saint's dedication. Weakest of the three.
- **Puteoli Nabataean inscriptions, CIS II 157 and 158** — Nabataean dedications
  in a Roman port, and the texts name Dushara and Aretas IV rather than Rome; the
  only open translation is Latin.
- **The Namāra inscription, 328 CE** — famously contested readings, but Rome is
  not named and the link is inferential; no open page pairs the text with a
  published English translation.
- **A second Palmyrene record** — not pursued. `arabia-02-palmyrene-tariff`
  already holds that ground and diversifying was judged worth more.
- **Sites that refused to load, recorded as infrastructure facts:**
  `dasi.cnr.it` (Anubis), `arabianepigraphicnotes.org` (maintenance mode),
  `books.openedition.org` (Anubis), OCIANA's new host `ociana.osu.edu` (per-record
  pages serve, **search is verification-gated**).

# PERSIA — the `verbatim_with_provenance` slot fills, the `verbatim_machine_english` slot does not

## P1 — the Abnūn inscription (ŠAb), 243/4 CE, Middle Persian

**Proposed id `persia-05-abnun`.** Ābnūn, a courtier of Šābuhr I, records that
when the Romans came against Pārs and Pahlaw he vowed that if Šābuhr were
victorious and made them submissive he would found a fire; he then records that
Šābuhr smote them, that Romans fell into Persian captivity, and that the fire is
established as the fulfilled oblation. Barm-e Delak, Fārs; the fire-altar is now
at the Narenjestān, Shiraz. Discovered in the 1990s.

- **Transliteration, normalised transcription and English translation in parallel
  on one open page**, loaded and read by this session:
  `https://sites.uci.edu/sasanika/abnun-inscription/`
- **Why this is the record and not Paikuli.** It is the Gordian III campaign seen
  **from below** — not a king's monument but a courtier's private vow, dated by a
  Roman invasion. The corpus already holds the king's side of exactly this
  material in `persia-01-skz`, the Res Gestae Divi Saporis. Two records, one war,
  two social registers.
- **GLOSS-CAPABLE, `contested_reading`, and it is the cleanest in this hunt.**
  The page states in its own words that **five** published translations exist —
  Tavoosi & Frye (*BAI* 3, 1989), Gignoux (*Studia Iranica* 20, 1991), Livshits &
  Nikitin (*BAI* n.s. 5, 1991), MacKenzie (*BAI* 7, 1993), Skjærvø (*Studia
  Iranica* 21, 1992) — and that **"all five translations are at times
  significantly different from another"**. That is a published, reachable
  statement of uncertainty about the reading, which is the thing ruling 62's
  discount was taken for the absence of.
- **The weakness, stated.** The English on the page is Sasanika's rendering
  **after** MacKenzie rather than a verbatim reprint, so the published-translation
  test is met by citation-of-record. And **BAI 7 could not be reached at source**:
  `bulletinasiainstitute.org/vol7.htm` sits in an https→http redirect loop and
  JSTOR `stable/i24045519` is paywalled. Two of the five editions are in French.

**Rejected for this slot, and both are near misses worth naming.** **Paikuli
(NPi) §91**, Narseh recording that Caesar and the Romans stood toward him in
peace and friendship — Humbach & Skjærvø's 1983 edition prints Middle Persian and
Parthian beside the English and a full scan is open, but it is **a scan of a
Reichert book on a third-party site**, which is a copyright question rather than
a reachability one, and Skjærvø's own front matter says he has "been liberal in
the use of queries (?)" with the query sitting on the Roman clause. **M2 I**,
Mani dispatching Mār Addā into the Roman Empire — TITUS carries the Manichaean
script and transliteration, Asmussen 1975 pp. 21–23 is free on archive.org, and
it is a Persian religious movement acting ON Rome rather than reacting to it.
**It is a Turfan fragment.** F3's own rule assigns region by FINDSPOT — the reason
`eastasia-04-jingjiao-stele` is East Asia and not Persia — so M2 is an East Asia
record, and **so is `M 5794 I`, which F3 banked as a Persia candidate.** That
banked candidate is mis-regioned and F6 should settle it rather than curate it.

**And one blocker from F3 is now removed.** F3 banked the **Istanbul epitaph of
Khurdād** and noted that `parsigdatabase.com` has no per-record URL. Sasanika's
"Istanbul Sarcophagus" page carries transliteration and transcription at a
stable per-record URL, so the candidate is materially more curatable than F3
believed. **One discrepancy to resolve first:** Sasanika dates it no later than
Bahrām V, fifth century; F3's note has early 8th to late 10th.

# SOUTH ASIA — the `verbatim_with_provenance` slot fills, the `verbatim_machine_english` slot does not, and the shape is Persia's exactly

## S1 — Bṛhatsaṃhitā 2.14, Varāhamihira, c. 550 CE, Sanskrit

**Proposed id `southasia-05-brihatsamhita`.** Varāhamihira, laying down who may
be trusted to practise astral science, concedes the standing of the **Yavanas** —
the Greek-speaking Mediterranean world whose astral learning had reached India.
It is the corpus's first Sanskrit record and its first record of an Indian
intellectual **judging** the Mediterranean rather than trading with it.

- **Original text**, Devanagari with IAST beside it:
  `https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita-sanskrit/d/doc1218183.html`
  (Achyutānanda Jhā's Sanskrit, 2001). Archival second witness for the verbatim
  field: **H. Kern, *The Bṛhat Sañhitā of Varāha-Mihira*, Baptist Mission Press,
  Calcutta 1865**, open on archive.org as `bhatsahitavarha00kerngoog`,
  NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT.
- **THE VERSE NUMBER IS 2.14 AND ONE OPEN TRANSLATION CALLS IT 15.** The hunt's
  first report gave the original-text URL as `doc1218184` and checking caught the
  off-by-one before it reached a record: **doc1218184 is 2.15 and contains
  neither `yavana` nor `mleccha`** — it is about not putting questions to a man
  whose judgement has been clouded. The Yavana verse is **doc1218183 = 2.14**.
  Iyer's English numbers the same sentence **verse 15**; Sastri & Bhat number it
  **II.14**, agreeing with the Sanskrit. Cite it as **Bṛhatsaṃhitā 2.14 (= Iyer
  ch. 2 v. 15)** with the offset stated, or a reviewer comparing the two open
  sources will read the record as misattributed.
- **GLOSS-CAPABLE, `contested_reading`, and both sides are open, published and
  verified at the page by this session.** The same six words are construed two
  incompatible ways:

```
N. Chidambaram Iyer, 1884, ch. 2 v. 15
  wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/brihat-samhita/d/doc226718.html -- read at source
  "Even the Mlecchas and the Yavanas (Greeks) who have well-studied the
   science are respected as Rsis."
                                              TWO GROUPS, coordinated

Subrahmanya Sastri & M. Ramakrishna Bhat, 1946, Adhyaya II sloka 14, p. 19
  archive.org/details/VarahamihirasBrihatSamhitaByVSubrahmanyaSastri
                                                          -- read at source
  "The Yavanas are of low origin. When this science has come to stay with
   them and when such astrologers are worshipped as sages, how much more
   should an astrologer of the Brahmin origin be?"
                                              ONE GROUP; low birth PREDICATED
                                              of the Yavanas, concessively
```

  On Iyer's reading Varāhamihira lists barbarians alongside Greeks. On Sastri &
  Bhat's he concedes that the Greeks **are** the barbarians and honours them
  anyway. **That is a disagreement about how to read the sentence, not about how
  to render it**, and the verse-number divergence travels with it as one
  editorial split. Publisher: V. B. Soobbiah & Sons, Bangalore City, 1946.
- **Cite archive.org, not the blog.** The same 1946 book and Bhat's 1981 Motilal
  Banarsidass revision both circulate as unauthorised uploads on
  `astrofoxx.wordpress.com`. The hunt read the 1946 one there first and then
  relocated it on archive.org precisely so this project would not have to cite
  it.
- **Kern's own English went nowhere and that is recorded rather than left
  looking checked.** `bhatsahitavarha00kerngoog` is Kern's **Sanskrit** edition,
  not his translation; his English is serialised across *JRAS* n.s. IV–VII
  (1870–75), not found on archive.org and abstract-only on Cambridge Core.

**Rejected for this slot, ranked and named.** **Nasik Cave 17, the Yavana
Indragnidatta** (EI VIII, 90–91) and the **Karle Yavana pillar inscriptions**
(EI VII no. 7, pp. 55–56; EI XVIII no. 36, pp. 325–329) — roughly half the named
pillar donors of a great Buddhist hall declare themselves Yavanas while carrying
Indian names, which is the "is this donor actually Greek" question in its purest
form. Both rejected because **the hunt located the volumes and their tables of
contents but never reached the inscription bodies**, and a record cannot be
written against a page nobody opened. **Neṭunalvāṭai 101–107** (Yavana-made
figural lamps in a Pandya bedchamber) and **Mullaippāṭṭu 59–66** (Yavana
bodyguards and mute Mleccha guards in a king's night camp) — both verified open
in Tamil at Project Madurai with open English, and both rejected for duplicating
the Akanānūru's language when Sanskrit is absent from the corpus entirely.
**Yavanajātaka of Sphujidhvaja** — carries the most famous contested reading in
Indian chronology, Mak having shown Pingree's date and several key readings to be
his own unsupported emendations; kept as the alternate if S1 fails.
**Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta XI**, Brahmagupta attacking the *Romaka-siddhānta* —
attractive for filling the late window at 628 CE, rejected because the Rome-touch
is second-order: he is criticising an Indian astronomer's version of a doctrine
that carries a Roman name.

### Failure mode 2 — the untranslated layer exists and the ORIGINAL cannot be reached

**Bhaṭṭotpala's Vivṛti on Bṛhatsaṃhitā 2.14, Kashmir, 966 CE.** Utpala glossing
Varāhamihira's Yavana verse for a tenth-century Sanskrit readership, and the most
important surviving witness to the lost Sanskrit Yavana astrological literature.
**No English translation exists**, and the negative is checkable because two near
misses do exist: Utpala's *Praśnajñāna* has an English translation and
Brahmagupta's *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* with Utpala's commentary was translated by
Sengupta in 1934 — **different works both**. M.R. Bhat's 1981 translation renders
the root text *using* Utpala's notes and does not translate Utpala.

**REFUSED ON THE SOURCE SIDE.** Two open Devanagari editions exist — Tripāṭhī
(Varanaseya Sanskrit Vishvavidyalaya) and **Sudhākara Dvivedī, Vizianagram
Sanskrit Series 18, CC0**, the edition scholarship conventionally cites. Neither
body text could be reached. This session went at the Dvivedī volume directly:
its `fulltext/inside.php` host **returned HTTP 403 to the fetch proxy**, and its
`_djvu.txt` came back with **adhyāya 2 unreachable, `यवन` absent from every
readable portion, and the Devanagari OCR "significantly corrupted throughout —
many akṣaras misread, vowel marks displaced".** From the Tripāṭhī volume's own
contents list, adhyāya 2 runs pp. 12–24 and verse 14 should fall near pp. 17–19,
scan pages ≈47–49. **That is an inference from page density, not a reading of the
gloss, and this document will not let it be written up as one.**

**Aṅgavijjā, Prakrit, c. 4th century, ed. Muni Puṇyavijaya, Prakrit Text Society,
Banaras, 1957** — a Jain divination manual that catalogues the coinage actually
circulating in Kushana-era India, naming **dīnāra** (from *denarius*),
**sateraka** (the Greek *stater*) and a **jonika**, a Greek woman. The
Roman-period economy seen from inside a text with no interest whatever in Rome,
which is what would make it good evidence. Open on archive.org
(`in.ernet.dli.2015.321409`), Devanagari, 486 pp. **No English translation found:
seven archive.org items, all Prakrit or Hindi; DOAJ returns zero.**

wisdomlib essay citing "(Angavijja, 1957:233)" for a different passage — **and
nobody has located the page carrying dīnāra, sateraka or jonika.** No clean
Unicode edition surfaced on any route tried.

## E1 — Tang Huiyao 唐會要 99, 拂菻國, compiled 961 CE. **TAKE THIS ONE.**

**Proposed id `eastasia-07-tang-huiyao`.** An institutional compendium built from
Tang court archives, textually independent of the two Tang histories at points:
Fulin north of the Western Sea; four hundred walled cities; palace pillars of
rock-crystal and glass; twelve joint ministers; election and deposition by merit
and after portents; water-driven palace cooling and a mechanical timepiece; and
the 643 embassy of King Boduoli with red glass — **the same embassy
`eastasia-06-cefu-yuangui` records from the other compendium**, which makes the
two records a parallel-witness pair rather than a repetition.

- **F4_CANDIDATES HAS THE KANRIPO CLASS WRONG BY OMISSION, AND THIS IS THE
  CORRECTION.** It recorded only `zh.wikisource.org/wiki/唐會要/卷099`. Kanripo
  carries the text as **`KR2m0002`** — 會要 works sit in **KR2m, 政書類**, not in
  KR2k, the geography class where the other two candidates live.
  **Verified by this session at
  `raw.githubusercontent.com/kanripo/KR2m0002/master/KR2m0002_099.txt`:**
  `#+PROPERTY: JUAN 卷九十九` present, the 拂菻 lemma present at roughly 1,200
  characters, **spanning `<pb:KR2m0002_WYG_099-24b>` through
  `<pb:KR2m0002_WYG_099-25b>`.**
- **Two independent open witnesses**, which is what no other candidate in this
  hunt has: Wikisource and Kanripo. Their lemma lists agree with minor graphic
  variants in three country names, so **the curator's hand-capture can be checked
  against a second edition** — the first record in this corpus for which that is
  true.
- **No file-number offset.** File number equals juan number for all three East
  Asia candidates, against the runbook's warning. Recorded so nobody re-derives
  it.
- **The recension is the Wenyuange Siku Quanshu** and the page marker says so, as
  with records 34 and 35. Same declared, uncollated boundary: the Qianlong
  editors are known to have altered passages about foreign peoples and this is a
  register of foreign peoples.
- **The English battery, re-run today, came back negative on every named source.**
  Hirth 1885 checked against the book's own contents and lettered source list
  (pp. 35–36) — he translates fifteen texts and **the Tang Huiyao is not among
  them**. Hirth & Rockhill 1911; Yule 1866; Fordham's Internet East Asian History
  Sourcebook (read through its Silk Road Seattle mirror, and it reproduces
  exactly Hirth's fifteen); Silk Road Seattle; silkroadfoundation.org;
  silk-road.com; HathiTrust; Cambridge Core; ScienceDirect; DOAJ; eScholarship;
  academia.edu; ResearchGate; Google Books; JSTOR open. **archive.org full-text
  search on `"Tang Huiyao" AND "Fulin"` returned ZERO results.**
- **The caveat that goes into the record.** Its content runs closely parallel to
  the Jiu Tangshu 198 and Xin Tangshu 221 Fulin accounts, **both of which Hirth
  translates and both openly reachable**. That is a parallel-witness overlap, not
  a translation of the Tang Huiyao — but it is the honest weakness and it belongs
  in `notes`.

## E2 — Taiping Huanyuji 太平寰宇記 184, 大秦國, c. 980 CE. **LIST B, and do not take it.**

Verified standing: `KR2k0004_184.txt`, `JUAN 184`, the 大秦國 lemma second of
twelve, about 4,626 characters. No reachable English. **It qualifies and it is a
poor showcase.** F4 called it a weak add on content grounds; today's check makes

~1,693 characters are Hou Hanshu-derived matter that **Hirth translates in full

Jingxingji, whose fragments Akin translated in 1999–2000 (login-gated) and
possibly Yule (unverified); and the tail is a commodity gloss stitched from
Baopuzi, Guangya and a Cao Zhi poem. **A machine translation of it would largely
